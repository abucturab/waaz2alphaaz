# Lisan-ud-Dawat

> Summary: Lisan-ud-Dawat is the liturgical and community language of the Dawoodi Bohra Muslim community, a Gujarati-Arabic-Persian creole/mixed language used in religious, literary, and daily life contexts.

## What It Is

Lisan-ud-Dawat (also spelled Lisān al-Daʿwa, Lisan-ul-Dawat, or simply LuD) is a language spoken exclusively by the Dawoodi Bohra community, a Shia Ismaili Muslim sect headquartered in Surat, Gujarat, India. It is not a dialect of any single language but rather a **mixed language** or **creole** that emerged from centuries of contact between Arabic and Gujarati, with significant Persian and some Hindustani influence.

The language occupies an unusual typological position: its grammatical skeleton and everyday spoken form are rooted in Gujarati, while its vocabulary draws heavily from Arabic and Farsi (Persian) — particularly for religious, administrative, legal, and intellectual concepts. The result is a language that looks partially like Arabic in its written form and sounds partially like Gujarati in its spoken form, yet is distinct from both.

## Why It Matters in This Domain

This project (waaz2alphaaz) targets the transcription and processing of Bohra religious discourse — specifically the *waaz* (sermon) tradition delivered in Lisan-ud-Dawat. Accurate ASR (Automatic Speech Recognition) and transcription require understanding the unique phonological, lexical, and orthographic features of this language, which do not map cleanly onto either Arabic or Gujarati NLP tooling.

## Key Principles

- **Not a dialect**: Lisan-ud-Dawat is not a dialect of Arabic, Gujarati, Persian, or Urdu. It is its own community language with its own norms, though it draws from all of them.
- **Code-switching is structural**: Switching between Gujarati-base grammar and Arabic/Persian vocabulary is not incidental — it is the defining feature of the language.
- **Religious register is dominant**: Most textual and audio resources in LuD are religious in nature (sermons, prayers, elegies, religious literature).
- **Script ≠ spoken phonology**: The Arabic-derived script does not represent all Gujarati-derived sounds natively; special diacritics and conventions bridge the gap (see [Script page](lisan_ud_dawat_script.md)).
- **Community-internal**: The language is not taught in public schools or recognized by any government body. Transmission is entirely within the Bohra community.
- **Oral dominance**: Much knowledge and religious content is transmitted orally; transcription resources are sparse.

## Etymology of the Name

"Lisan-ud-Dawat" is an Arabic phrase:
- **لسان** (*lisān*) = "tongue" / "language"
- **الدعوة** (*al-daʿwa*) = "the Da'wa" — the religious mission/organization of the Ismaili Fatimid tradition

Together: **"The language of the Da'wa"** — i.e., the language of the Fatimid missionary movement and its institutional successor, the Dawoodi Bohra religious hierarchy (led by the *Dai al-Mutlaq*). The name itself signals that this is not merely an ethnic tongue but a language with deliberate religious-institutional identity.

## History & Origin

### Fatimid Roots (10th–12th century CE)
The Da'wa (missionary organization) of the Fatimid Caliphate (Cairo, Egypt) sent missionaries (*duat*) to Gujarat, India, in the 11th–12th centuries CE. Arabic was the language of the Fatimid court and religious texts. These missionaries encountered Gujarati-speaking populations.

### Gujarat Settlement (12th–16th century)
As the Ismaili mission took root in Gujarat, a bilingual community formed. Over generations, the community began using a mixed form of language that retained Arabic vocabulary for religious/legal concepts while adopting Gujarati grammar for everyday speech. This is the emergence point of Lisan-ud-Dawat.

### Post-Fatimid period (after 1171 CE)
After the fall of the Fatimid Caliphate, the Da'wa moved its seat to Yemen and later to India. The language continued evolving in isolation from mainstream Arabic, absorbing Gujarati grammatical structures more deeply.

### Consolidation (16th century onwards)
When the seat of the Dai al-Mutlaq moved to Gujarat (Surat) permanently, Lisan-ud-Dawat became the definitive community language. A literary tradition developed: religious texts, poetry (*marsiya*, *qasida*), and *risala* (epistles) were composed in LuD.

### Modern period (20th–21st century)
The Dawoodi Bohra diaspora has spread LuD to East Africa, the UK, North America, Southeast Asia, and the Gulf. In all diaspora communities, LuD continues to be used in religious contexts. The 52nd and 53rd *Dai al-Mutlaq*, Syedna Tahir Saifuddin and Syedna Mohammed Burhanuddin, composed extensively in LuD. The current (54th) Dai, Syedna Mufaddal Saifuddin, also uses LuD in religious discourse.

## Phonology

Lisan-ud-Dawat's phonology reflects its dual heritage:

**Gujarati-inherited sounds:**
- Aspirated stops: /pʰ/, /bʱ/, /tʰ/, /dʱ/, /kʰ/, /ɡʱ/
- Retroflex consonants: /ʈ/, /ɖ/, /ɳ/, /ɽ/
- The mid central vowel /ə/ (schwa) common in Indo-Aryan languages
- Nasalized vowels

**Arabic-inherited sounds (present in learned/religious vocabulary):**
- Pharyngeal fricatives: /ħ/ (ح) and /ʕ/ (ع)
- Uvular stop: /q/ (ق)
- Emphatic consonants: /sˤ/ (ص), /dˤ/ (ض), /tˤ/ (ط), /ðˤ/ (ظ)
- Glottal stop: /ʔ/ (hamza, ء)

**Key phonological notes for ASR:**
- Many Arabic phonemes are phonemically merged or modified in spoken LuD for Gujarati-speaker convenience. For example, Arabic /q/ may be realized as /k/, and emphatic consonants may lose their pharyngealization in informal speech.
- Vowel length is phonemic (inherited from Arabic).
- The language is tonal in some interpretations — the *waaz* (sermon) style has distinctive prosodic patterns used for emphasis and liturgical effect. <!-- unverified -->

## Registers and Variation

- **Religious/liturgical register**: Heavily Arabic-dominant in vocabulary; used in waaz, prayers, and religious texts.
- **Everyday/domestic register**: More Gujarati-dominant; used in daily conversation within the community.
- **Literary/poetic register**: Rich mixture; the *marsiya* (elegies for Imam Husain) and *qasida* (panegyrics) form a well-developed corpus.
- **Waaz register**: The sermon tradition (*waaz*) blends all registers and includes Quranic quotation, Hadith citation, Persian poetry, and Gujarati narrative — making it linguistically the most complex.

## Common Misunderstandings

- **"It's just Gujarati with Arabic words"**: This undersells the depth of integration. The vocabulary replacement is systematic, and the script is a fully different writing system from Gujarati.
- **"It's a dialect of Arabic"**: The grammatical structure is Indo-Aryan, not Semitic.
- **"It's the same as Urdu"**: While both have Arabic/Persian vocabulary overlaid on an Indo-Aryan base, Urdu's base is Khariboli Hindi (not Gujarati), and the scripts and communities are entirely distinct.
- **"All Bohras speak it natively"**: Among diaspora youth especially, LuD may be a heritage/second language, with English or the local language being the primary tongue.

## Examples

A typical phrase illustrates the mixed nature:

> **"Hazrat Imam Husain (as) ni shahadat nu matam aapne bade adab thi karvanu chhe."**
> (We must perform the mourning of the martyrdom of Hazrat Imam Husain with great respect.)

- *Hazrat Imam Husain (as)* — Arabic honorific + Arabic name
- *shahadat* — Arabic (martyrdom)
- *matam* — Arabic/Persian (mourning)
- *aapne ... karvanu chhe* — Gujarati grammar
- *adab* — Arabic/Persian (respect)
- *bade* — Persian/Urdu influence (big/great)
